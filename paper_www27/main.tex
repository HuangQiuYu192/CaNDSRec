\title{Disentangling Direction, Sequence Scale, and Item Scale in Sequential Recommendation}
\begin{abstract}
Dot-product sequential recommenders entangle directional compatibility with two radial quantities: a sequence-specific logit scale and a candidate-specific ranking factor. We study this final matching geometry through a controlled family of raw dot, globally scaled dot, sequence-only, item-only, and joint normalized scores. We prove that sequence-only normalization preserves every within-user rank for a fixed state, while item normalization can change candidate order; normalized scoring also removes the direct radial gradient on each normalized side. These facts yield discriminating tests rather than a generic cosine claim. On Sports and Toys, a globally scaled dot-product control selected on independent validation data improves the raw baseline but remains below sequence-only and joint normalization across three held-out seeds. A frozen Beauty sweep preserves sequence-side top-10 sets while item-side scaling progressively changes them, and a matched training trace shows numerically zero sequence-radial gradient energy for joint scoring. The evidence supports a bounded interpretation: directional normalization is a useful training inductive bias under these matched protocols, not a universal similarity rule or a long-tail solution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sequential recommendation predicts the next interaction from an ordered history. Recurrent, self-attentive, and masked-item models differ in how they construct a sequence state~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec,kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}, but commonly finish with a dot product $s_{ui}=\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i$. Writing $\bm h_u=\|\bm h_u\|\widehat{\bm h}_u$ and $\bm e_i=\|\bm e_i\|\widehat{\bm e}_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
s_{ui}=\underbrace{\|\bm h_u\|\|\bm e_i\|}_{\text{radial scale}}\underbrace{\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_i}_{\text{directional compatibility}}.\label{eq:dot}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:dot} mixes two distinct quantities. Direction measures whether a history and an item are compatible; radius controls score scale. This distinction is consequential for learning, but its inference-time implication is subtle. If only $\bm h_u$ is unit-normalized, then every candidate score for the same user is divided by the same positive $\|\bm h_u\|$. The ordering is therefore unchanged for a frozen representation. A test-time sequence-cosine re-ranker cannot improve a deterministic full-sort ranking except through numerical ties.

This exact invariance creates a useful research question. Sequence normalization is routinely described as a similarity choice, yet a benefit after retraining cannot be attributed to direct test-time re-ranking. It may instead arise from at least three competing mechanisms: (i) replacing sample-dependent sequence scale with a global temperature, (ii) changing the sequence-side training path by removing radial gradients, or (iii) changing the candidate order through item-side normalization. Treating all normalized scores as one method obscures these alternatives.

We therefore study a controlled family of final scoring geometries. \cands{} denotes the joint angular member, $\tau\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_i$, which changes neither the encoder nor the candidate universe. Sequence-only normalization is not presented as a weaker version of \cands{}: it is the \,\emph{theory-isolation control} for which frozen rank invariance is exact. Joint normalization is an \,\emph{endpoint configuration} that may additionally change the candidate order. Our question is consequently narrower and testable: \emph{which aspects of score normalization change inference ranking, which aspects change training dynamics, and which are necessary for improved sequential ranking?}

Our contributions are an evidence-driven decomposition of that question:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item We formalize a direction--scale decomposition of the final score. Sequence-side normalization is rank-invariant for fixed representations but constrains the sequence scoring gradient to the tangent space; item-side normalization can alter candidate order.
\item We specify a score-geometry control suite that separates global logit scale (\scaledot{}), sequence-side training geometry, and item-side candidate reordering. Each claimed mechanism has an associated falsifiable checkpoint, endpoint, or trajectory prediction.
\item We report matched-seed endpoint and frozen-score evidence currently available, and make the remaining decisive controls explicit rather than inferring a mechanism from final metrics alone. Frequency-stratified results are retained only as a boundary analysis, not as the paper objective.
\end{itemize}

This framing turns a small scoring change into a falsifiable study of training geometry. It also gives a practical boundary: if normalization moves deep-rank targets closer without improving their top-$K$ positions, a separate fine-ranking or evidence module is needed rather than another unexamined normalization rule.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Sequential next-item models}
Sequential models improve the state constructed from an interaction prefix. GRU4Rec uses recurrent updates~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec}, SASRec uses causal self-attention~\cite{kang2018sasrec}, and BERT4Rec uses a masked-item objective~\cite{sun2019bert4rec}. These models mainly differ in how they encode order, recency, and dependencies in the history. In contrast, this paper holds the encoder family fixed and asks what geometry should compare its resulting state with the candidate catalogue. This distinction is important for attribution: a gain caused by a new history encoder is not evidence about the final scoring rule, and a scoring change should be tested without changing the data split or candidate universe.

\subsection{Normalized representations and metric geometry}
Unit-normalized representations have been used to make angular relations explicit in classification and representation learning~\cite{wang2017normface,davidson2018hyperspherical}. In particular, NormFace analyzes the scale constraints created by normalized-softmax training rather than treating normalization as an unqualified improvement~\cite{wang2017normface}. Alignment--uniformity analysis similarly pairs a geometric decomposition with measurable quantities and controlled downstream tests~\cite{wang2020alignment}. These studies motivate the present methodology, but do not resolve the sequential full-sort setting in which sequence and candidate radii play asymmetric roles. Our contribution is consequently not cosine similarity itself; it is a control design that separates frozen ranking, sequence-side training geometry, global scale, and item-side reordering.

\subsection{Geometry analyses in recommendation}
Recent recommendation studies use theory and measurement to identify geometric limitations, including embedding collapse under model scaling~\cite{guo2024collapse} and anisotropy in pretrained text embeddings for sequential recommendation~\cite{zhang2024whitenrec}. Their common pattern is to begin with an observable representation property, formalize its consequence, and test a targeted intervention. We follow this pattern but focus on the final matching score of an ID-based sequential recommender. Our controlled family asks whether an apparent benefit of normalization is attributable to explicit temperature, a changed optimization path, or a changed candidate ranking.

\subsection{Score calibration and frequency effects}
Popularity-aware recommendation studies show that frequency can influence accuracy, exposure, and learned ranking~\cite{steck2011popularity,abdollahpouri2017controlling}. This motivates frequency-stratified evaluation, but it does not imply that an embedding norm is a popularity proxy or that removing norms helps rare targets. We therefore treat popularity only as a diagnostic stratification: active items are partitioned using training interactions, and group results are reported as a boundary of a geometry intervention. \cands{} is neither a popularity penalty nor a tail-augmentation method; it is one member of a controlled final-score geometry family.

\section{Method and Analysis}
\subsection{Task and a controlled score family}
Let $\mathcal I$ denote the catalogue and let $\bm x_u=(i_1,\ldots,i_L)$ be a chronological prefix for user $u$. A causal sequential encoder $f_\theta$ maps the prefix to $\bm h_u\in\mathbb R^d$, and each item $i$ has an embedding $\bm e_i\in\mathbb R^d$. Let $N(\bm v)=\bm v/(\|\bm v\|_2+\epsilon)$, where $\epsilon$ is only a numerical safeguard. Rather than compare a dot product with one cosine variant, we study the following family under an otherwise identical encoder, loss, data split, and candidate universe:
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{rcll}
s^{\mathrm{dot}}_{ui}&=&\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i, & \text{raw baseline},\\
s^{\mathrm{scale}}_{ui}&=&\tau\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i, & \text{global-scale control},\\
s^{\mathrm{seq}}_{ui}&=&\tau N(\bm h_u)^\top\bm e_i, & \text{sequence-only control},\\
s^{\mathrm{item}}_{ui}&=&\tau\bm h_u^\top N(\bm e_i), & \text{item-only control},\\
s^{\mathrm{joint}}_{ui}&=&\tau N(\bm h_u)^\top N(\bm e_i), & \text{joint angular score (\cands{})}.
\end{array}\label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
For a positive next item $i^+$, the matched implementation optimizes the same full-sort cross-entropy configuration as the baseline,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\log\frac{\exp(s_{ui^+})}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}\exp(s_{uj})}.\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The scalar $\tau>0$ controls logit concentration and gradient scale, but cannot change a within-user ordering when it multiplies all candidates equally. The family changes neither history encoder, item identities, sequence length, nor asymptotic full-sort complexity. Its purpose is attribution: $s^{\mathrm{scale}}$ tests an explicit global-scale explanation, $s^{\mathrm{seq}}$ isolates sequence-side training geometry, $s^{\mathrm{item}}$ isolates candidate-side ordering changes, and $s^{\mathrm{joint}}$ is the empirical configuration of interest.

\paragraph{Partial normalization as a screening control.} To probe whether a partial removal of radial freedom reproduces a full normalized endpoint, we use $N_p(\bm v)=\bm v/(\|\bm v\|_2+\epsilon)^p$. The completed Beauty screen fixes $\tau=10$ and evaluates the available $(p_h,p_e)\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1),(.5,.5),(1,.5),(.5,1)\}$ configurations on one development seed. It is deliberately a mechanism screen rather than a selected multi-seed comparison; no monotonic performance claim is assumed.

\subsection{Rank invariance and candidate-side asymmetry}
Consider first sequence-only normalization, $s^{\mathrm{seq}}_{ui}=\tau\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i$. For a fixed nonzero state $\bm h_u$, $s^{\mathrm{seq}}_{ui}=\frac{\tau}{\|\bm h_u\|_2}s_{ui}$. The multiplier is positive and independent of $i$; it cannot change the within-user order. This property is exact in real arithmetic and motivates a frozen-representation counterfactual in our experiments.
\begin{proposition}[Sequence-side rank invariance]
For any nonzero $\bm h_u$ and arbitrary candidate vectors $\{\bm e_i\}_{i\in\mathcal I}$, $\operatorname{rank}_i(\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i)=\operatorname{rank}_i(\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i)$, up to ties.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For all candidates $i$, $\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i=(1/\|\bm h_u\|_2)\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i$. Multiplication by a common positive scalar preserves every pairwise score inequality.
\end{proof}

Item-side normalization is not invariant in this way because $\|\bm e_i\|_2$ differs across candidates. For target $t$ and competitor $p$, set $c_t=\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_t$ and $c_p=\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_p$. If $c_t>c_p>0$, angular scoring chooses $t$ but the dot product chooses $p$ exactly when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|\bm e_p\|_2}{\|\bm e_t\|_2}>\frac{c_t}{c_p}.\label{eq:reversal}
\end{equation}
The sequence norm cancels, making this a candidate-side radial effect. Eq.~\ref{eq:reversal} is not a superiority claim: candidate radius may encode useful calibration information. It instead establishes why sequence-only and item-only normalization must be analyzed separately.
\begin{proposition}For nonzero representations, \cands{} ranks $t$ above $p$ if and only if $c_t>c_p$, and this order is invariant to independent positive rescaling of the sequence and item vectors.\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}The joint score in Eq.~\ref{eq:family} is the positive constant $\tau$ times $c_i$. Replacing either vector by a positive multiple leaves its unit vector unchanged. Thus the score difference has the same sign as $c_t-c_p$, independently of all positive radii.\end{proof}
This proposition identifies an invariance, not a superiority theorem. If norm encodes a useful property, dot-product ranking may be preferable. The empirical role of the next sections is to determine whether this restriction helps under matched training.

\subsection{Training geometry}
The rank invariance above is not an optimization invariance. For the dot-product full-softmax loss, with $p_{uj}$ denoting the softmax probability and $y_{uj}=\mathbf{1}[j=i^+]$, the item gradient is $\nabla_{\bm e_j}\mathcal L=(p_{uj}-y_{uj})\bm h_u$. Its radial projection is generally nonzero. Likewise, scaling $\bm h_u$ changes dot-product logits without changing its direction, so the sequence norm acts as a sample-dependent logit scale during training.
\begin{proposition}[Tangential normalized-score gradient]
Let $\bm z=\bm v/\|\bm v\|_2$ and let $\mathcal L$ be any differentiable loss that depends on $\bm v$ only through $\bm z$. Then
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L=\frac{1}{\|\bm v\|_2}\left(\bm I-\bm z\bm z^\top\right)\nabla_{\bm z}\mathcal L,
\qquad \bm v^\top\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L=0.\label{eq:tangent}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}For nonzero $\bm v$, differentiating $\bm z=\bm v/\|\bm v\|_2$ gives the Jacobian $\|\bm v\|_2^{-1}(\bm I-\bm z\bm z^\top)$. Applying the chain rule yields the first equality. The matrix in parentheses is an orthogonal projection onto the tangent space of the unit sphere, hence it annihilates $\bm z$. Since $\bm v=\|\bm v\|_2\bm z$, the inner product with $\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L$ is zero.\end{proof}
Equation~\ref{eq:tangent} applies to $\bm v=\bm h_u$ for sequence-only normalization and to both $\bm h_u$ and $\bm e_i$ for joint normalization. Thus, sequence normalization can change training even though it cannot change the frozen ranking in Proposition~1. The $1/\|\bm v\|_2$ factor also changes directional update scale in the unconstrained parameterization. This does not guarantee higher metrics: radius can contain useful information, and other paths through the encoder or optimizer can affect norms indirectly. Matched endpoint tests and direct gradient diagnostics are consequently both necessary.

\begin{proposition}[Cosine-logit capacity]
For a catalogue of $M$ items and temperature-scaled cosine logits
$\ell_{ui}\in[-\tau,\tau]$, the per-example full-softmax cross-entropy loss
is bounded below by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{u}\geq \log\!\left(1+(M-1)e^{-2\tau}\right).\label{eq:logitbound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The positive logit cannot exceed $\tau$, while every negative logit is at
least $-\tau$. The largest attainable positive softmax probability is therefore
$[1+(M-1)e^{-2\tau}]^{-1}$. Taking its negative logarithm gives
Eq.~\ref{eq:logitbound}.
\end{proof}
This bound makes the role of temperature explicit. Unit normalization bounds all logit gaps by $2\tau$; $\tau$ is therefore an explicit capacity and calibration control, not an incidental hyperparameter. It cannot alter a fixed user's angular ordering, but it changes loss concentration and gradients during training.

The score family makes competing explanations testable rather than rhetorical. We use the following prediction--test map.

First, for a fixed representation we compare dot scores with sequence-normalized scores under the identical history mask. Proposition~1 predicts identical target ranks and top-$K$ lists up to numerical ties. We also normalize only item embeddings; its lower top-$K$ overlap quantifies candidate-side asymmetry rather than treating it as an optimization effect.

Second, we measure radial-gradient energy at the final matching head, score standard deviation, predictive entropy, and top-one/top-two margin after training. Equation~\ref{eq:tangent} predicts a zero direct radial component for normalized inputs. These distributional measures are descriptive diagnostics, not standalone causal proof.

Third, the matched Beauty trajectory records validation ranking, sequence norm, radial-gradient energy, and score entropy at every epoch for 60 epochs. It uses one seed as a direct diagnostic of Eq.~\ref{eq:tangent}; it is not used for model selection or cross-seed inference. Finally, paired rank transitions record movements among $1$--$5$, $6$--$10$, $11$--$20$, $21$--$50$, $51$--$100$, and $>100$. A model can improve deep retrieval while failing to improve top-$10$ ranking. Collectively, these controls distinguish a frozen-score property from a retrained endpoint effect; they do not identify a causal data-generating process.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\subsection{Data and evaluation split}
We process Amazon Beauty, Sports, and Toys into chronological user sequences. For every eligible user, the latest interaction is held out for test and the preceding interaction for validation; all earlier interactions form training prefixes. Table~\ref{tab:data} describes the processed data. The three domains differ in catalogue size, density, and maximum observed sequence length, so a result that repeats across them is less likely to be a single-catalogue artifact.

We evaluate by full-sort ranking over the complete catalogue after masking observed training items according to the standard sequential-evaluation protocol. For a target rank $r_u$, $\mathrm{Recall@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal U|}\sum_u\mathbb{1}[r_u\leq K]$ and $\mathrm{NDCG@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal U|}\sum_u\mathbb{1}[r_u\leq K]/\log_2(r_u+1)$. We report both metrics at cutoffs $5$, $10$, $20$, $50$, and $100$, using NDCG@10 as the primary compact metric in the main tables. Head, mid, and tail are global, training-only partitions of active items sorted by training interaction count. The groups contain equal numbers of \emph{items}, not equal numbers of test examples; this prevents test leakage and avoids changing a target's group according to the test split.

\subsection{Matched score-geometry comparison}
All score-family members share architecture, chronological split, optimizer, batch size, early stopping rule, and random seed. The initial SASRec setup has two Transformer layers, 256 hidden units, two attention heads, inner size 1024, and maximum history length 50. The only intended method difference is the selected row of Eq.~\ref{eq:family}. Three seeds (2023, 2024, and 2025) are matched one-to-one for completed endpoint comparisons. We report mean and standard deviation, paired seed deltas, and the fraction of positive deltas.

The test set is never used to choose a checkpoint, $\tau$, normalization power, or encoder-specific configuration. For the cross-domain \scaledot{} control, an independent development seed (2026) selects $\tau$ using validation NDCG@10, after which the choice is locked for seeds 2023--2025. The sequence and joint rows use the fixed $\tau=10$ matched score-family protocol. The Beauty partial-normalization screen is explicitly labelled as a fixed-$\tau$ development diagnostic rather than a selected endpoint result.

\subsection{Pre-specified separation controls}
The endpoint study is organized as an evidence ladder rather than a single ``dot versus cosine'' comparison:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{P0: global-scale control.} Compare dot, validation-selected \scaledot{}, sequence-only, item-only, and joint scores on Beauty with matched seeds. This control decides whether an explicit global multiplier can account for normalized endpoints.
\item \textbf{P1: training-path control.} Save matched checkpoints at fixed epochs and compare validation ranking, radial-gradient energy, norm distributions, and score entropy. This tests the predicted change in optimization path rather than inferring it only from a final checkpoint.
\item \textbf{P2: radial dose-response.} Evaluate partial normalization powers with independently validation-selected temperatures. A non-monotonic response is admissible; the purpose is to map the boundary, not to force a monotonicity claim.
\item \textbf{P3: encoder transfer.} Repeat the smallest decisive control suite on two structurally distinct sequential encoders, initially a frequency-aware encoder and a contrastive-learning encoder. \TODO{lock exact encoder versions and datasets before execution}.
\end{enumerate}

The comparison is intentionally narrow. It tests attribution for a final-score intervention; it does not establish dominance over every recent sequential model. Changing the encoder, negative-sampling regime, preprocessing, and score geometry together would make an apparent gain hard to interpret.

\subsection{Mechanism protocol and reproducibility}
For each fixed test state, our diagnostic masks histories identically before comparing raw dot, sequence-normalized, and item-normalized scores. It records rank equality, top-$K$ overlap, score scale, entropy, margins, and transitions between rank bins. This is a frozen-score diagnostic, not a causal claim about retraining. We separately test the tangential-gradient prediction by differentiating only the final matching head, holding the complementary side fixed. Every endpoint record stores dataset, seed, checkpoint path, geometry, temperature, and command. Released scripts separate training, validation-only tuning, checkpoint analysis, and final aggregation so that an analysis script cannot silently alter a completed result.

\subsection{Mechanism evidence}
The decisive test of Proposition~1 uses the same frozen representation, history mask, target, and candidate catalogue. Across three Beauty seeds, replacing raw dot scores by sequence-normalized scores preserved the target rank for $99.91\%$--$99.96\%$ of test examples and preserved the entire top-$10$ list for approximately $99.99\%$. The small residual is attributable to finite-precision comparisons at near ties. Thus, a sequence-only score substitution is not a plausible explanation for the endpoint gain.

The same counterfactual exposes the candidate-side asymmetry. Normalizing item vectors reduced raw-versus-item-normalized top-$10$ Jaccard overlap to .71--.79, depending on the trained checkpoint. Item normalization consequently changes the candidate list, whereas sequence normalization does not. This distinction is why joint normalization should be evaluated with a separate temperature rather than described as a stronger version of the same inference-time operation.

\subsection{Direct gradient check and external decomposition}
The gradient prediction is observed directly rather than inferred from final embedding norms. Holding the candidate table fixed, the radial-energy fraction of the final sequence-state gradient was 3.25\% on average for dot-product SASRec across Beauty seeds, and numerically zero ($<4\times10^{-16}$) for sequence-only and joint normalization. Holding the encoder output fixed gives the corresponding item-side result: it is near zero for joint normalization but not for sequence-only normalization. These measurements match the asymmetric derivation in Eq.~\ref{eq:tangent}: sequence-only normalization constrains the sequence scoring path, while joint normalization constrains both final-score paths.

Temperature is necessary for this constraint to remain useful. A wide Beauty development grid over $\{.5,1,2,5,10,20,40\}$ selected $\tau=5$ for sequence-only and $\tau=10$ for joint normalization. A local validation-only refinement selected $\tau=4$ for sequence-only (NDCG@10=.0605) but did not improve the joint $\tau=10$ optimum (NDCG@10=.0608). Independent seeds confirmed that sequence $\tau=4$ and $\tau=5$ occupy a stable region: $\tau=4$ increased NDCG@10 by .0003 while decreasing Recall@10 by .0016. We therefore report a stable region rather than claiming a universal point optimum.

The paired rank-transition analysis clarifies the frequency boundary. For tail targets, sequence-only and joint normalization improved Recall@50 by .0088 and .0176, respectively, but changed Recall@10 by $-.0020$ and $-.0011$. Joint normalization moved 2.58\% of tail targets from ranks above 50 into ranks 11--50, yet its top-$10$ entry rate (.69\%) remained below its top-$10$ exit rate (.81\%). The geometry therefore improves coarse retrieval for some deep targets without guaranteeing their fine top-$K$ order.

We then use the pre-specified Yelp decomposition as an external check beyond short Amazon purchase sequences. Joint normalization is best on every reported Yelp metric, whereas item-only normalization is below the dot product. Thus the result supports neither a generic ``smaller norm'' explanation nor the view that normalizing items alone is sufficient. The dot-product L2 control also does not match joint normalization. These observations support joint score geometry as an empirical option while retaining sequence-scale calibration as the mechanism isolated by the frozen-rank theorem.

\subsection{Sensitivity and failure modes}
The current evidence does not support a transferable point value of $\tau$. The Beauty development experiment identifies an interior effective region, while external experiments retain independently selected protocols. Temperature must be tuned on validation data because its role is to set loss concentration after radial scale is constrained; selecting it on test data would invalidate the mechanism comparison. The final version will add the P0 and P2 selection records before making any statement about temperature robustness.

The matched seeds identify three failure modes. First, a post-hoc sequence-cosine replacement is rank-invariant and cannot reproduce a retrained endpoint gain. Second, item-only normalization is not a safe proxy for joint normalization because it removes candidate-specific scale from the ordering. Third, tail NDCG can decrease even when overall NDCG and deep recall improve. A practitioner seeking tail coverage should therefore add tail-specific evidence, objectives, or constraints rather than treating score normalization as a substitute for them.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Ethics}
The central interpretation is narrower than a generic claim for cosine similarity. The frozen-state counterfactual establishes one exact negative result: sequence-only normalization cannot improve a fixed deterministic ranking through post-hoc re-ranking. The direct radial-gradient check establishes a distinct geometric fact: normalization removes the direct radial component on the normalized scoring path. The held-out scale control shows that explicit temperature helps but does not account for the sequence-only or joint endpoints. The available partial screen rejects arbitrary radial shrinkage as a sufficient explanation. What remains open is transfer beyond the SASRec encoder family and the causal contribution of each training-path correlate.

The frequency result provides a concrete boundary. \cands{} neither reconstructs missing tail evidence nor guarantees tail exposure. The transition analysis shows that angular geometry can improve deep retrieval while failing to resolve local top-$K$ competition among sparse candidates. Items with few interactions may need content, graph structure, uncertainty estimates, or explicitly constrained objectives; none is supplied by directional calibration. The practical role of \cands{} is therefore a low-complexity geometric baseline or component, not a replacement for a tail-specific method.

There are also threats to validity. The fixed-checkpoint comparison controls sequence state, history mask, examples, and candidate universe but cannot identify a causal data-generation process. The completed study uses one encoder family, three Amazon domains plus Yelp, fixed hidden width, and a validation-selected \scaledot{} temperature protocol. It reports matched-seed variation and, on Beauty, within-seed paired user bootstrap intervals. These design choices make current attribution cleaner but limit external generalization; in particular, the trajectory and partial screen are single-seed diagnostics and cross-encoder transfer remains open.

We use anonymous public interaction IDs, no sensitive attributes, no review text, and no synthetic behavior. Data redistribution follows the original source terms. Offline relevance metrics do not measure marketplace exposure, seller impact, or user welfare; any deployment would require coverage, fairness, and stakeholder analyses beyond the scope of this study.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper provides a controlled template for studying direction and scale in sequential recommendation. It separates an exact frozen-ranking property of sequence normalization from the candidate-side ordering changes induced by item normalization, and turns global scale, training geometry, and candidate reordering into competing empirical explanations. Across the completed controls, global temperature accounts for part but not all of the endpoint gain; sequence-side normalization is a rank-invariant frozen intervention but a different training geometry; and joint normalization adds a candidate-side ordering constraint. The intended contribution is not a universal cosine-similarity prescription or a tail-recommendation method, but an evidence standard for explaining a small score-geometry intervention.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]

\item Both counterfactual scores share checkpoint, history mask, examples, and candidate universe.
\item Every endpoint row records data version, seed, checkpoint, and command.
\item Reported uncertainty includes matched-seed variation and, for Beauty joint normalization, paired user-level bootstrap intervals.
