\title{When Rank-Invariant Normalization Improves Sequential Recommendation: Training Geometry and Temperature Calibration}
\begin{abstract}
Dot-product sequential recommenders entangle sequence--item compatibility with the norms of the sequence state and candidate embedding. A central but overlooked consequence is that normalizing a emph{fixed} sequence state cannot change its within-user candidate ranking: it only rescales every candidate score by the same positive quantity. Why, then, can sequence normalization improve a recommender after training? We study this question through \cands{}, a parameter-free temperature-scaled angular scoring rule. We prove that sequence normalization removes the radial component of the final matching gradient and replaces a sample-dependent logit scale by an explicit global temperature; item normalization is asymmetric because it can alter candidate order. On Beauty, a validation-only temperature study finds stable effective regions around $\tau=4$--$5$ for sequence-only normalization and $\tau=10$ for joint normalization. Across three independent seeds, joint normalization improves SASRec from .0394 to .0460 NDCG@10, while a frozen-state counterfactual preserves sequence-normalized ranks for more than 99.9\% of examples. Score-scale, entropy, margin, and rank-transition diagnostics show that the gain is a training-geometry and calibration effect, not a post-hoc re-ranking effect. Frequency-stratified analyses are reported as a boundary: normalization improves overall ranking but does not by itself guarantee tail NDCG@10 gains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sequential recommendation predicts the next interaction from an ordered history. Recurrent, self-attentive, and masked-item models differ in how they construct a sequence state~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec,kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}, but commonly finish with a dot product $s_{ui}=\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i$. Writing $\bm h_u=\|\bm h_u\|\widehat{\bm h}_u$ and $\bm e_i=\|\bm e_i\|\widehat{\bm e}_i$ gives
\begin{equation}
s_{ui}=\underbrace{\|\bm h_u\|\|\bm e_i\|}_{\text{radial scale}}\underbrace{\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_i}_{\text{directional compatibility}}.\label{eq:dot}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:dot} mixes two distinct quantities. Direction measures whether a history and an item are compatible; radius controls score scale. This distinction is consequential for learning, but its inference-time implication is subtle. If only $\bm h_u$ is unit-normalized, then every candidate score for the same user is divided by the same positive $\|\bm h_u\|$. The ordering is therefore unchanged for a frozen representation. A test-time sequence-cosine re-ranker cannot improve a deterministic full-sort ranking except through numerical ties.

This exact invariance creates a useful research question. Sequence normalization is routinely described as a similarity choice, yet its benefit---when one occurs---cannot be attributed to direct test-time re-ranking. Instead, it must arise because the training objective and its gradients changed. In particular, dot-product training permits each sequence state to set a sample-dependent logit scale through $\|\bm h_u\|$, whereas sequence normalization removes that degree of freedom and leaves a global temperature to control concentration. Normalizing item embeddings is different: item radii are candidate-specific and their removal can change rankings. Treating the two operations as interchangeable obscures both the mechanism and the role of temperature.

We study this distinction with \cands{}, a parameter-free modification of a self-attentive sequential recommender. \cands{} uses a temperature-scaled angular score and changes neither the encoder nor the candidate universe. Our aim is not to claim that cosine similarity is universally better, or that normalization solves long-tail recommendation. Rather, we ask: \emph{when a rank-invariant sequence normalization improves a trained sequential recommender, what evidence establishes the training-geometry mechanism?}

Our contributions are an evidence-driven analysis of that question:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=2pt]
\item We formalize the asymmetry between sequence-side and item-side normalization. Sequence-side normalization is rank-invariant for fixed representations, but constrains the final matching gradient to the tangent space of the sequence sphere; item-side normalization can alter candidate order.
\item We derive the role of temperature as an explicit replacement for sample-dependent sequence scale, and formulate falsifiable diagnostics for frozen-score invariance, radial-gradient energy, score spread, entropy, and rank transitions.
\item We combine matched endpoint experiments with validation-only temperature selection and multi-seed checkpoint analyses. The evidence separates a training-geometry effect from a post-hoc score substitution, while reporting frequency-stratified limitations without making them the paper's objective.
\end{itemize}

This framing makes a small architectural change scientifically testable. It also gives a practical boundary: if normalization moves deep-rank targets closer without improving their top-$K$ positions, a separate fine-ranking or evidence module is needed rather than another unexamined normalization rule.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Sequential next-item models}
Sequential models improve the state constructed from an interaction prefix. GRU4Rec uses recurrent updates~\cite{hidasi2016gru4rec}, SASRec uses causal self-attention~\cite{kang2018sasrec}, and BERT4Rec uses a masked-item objective~\cite{sun2019bert4rec}. These models mainly differ in how they encode order, recency, and dependencies in the history. In contrast, this paper holds the encoder family fixed and asks what geometry should compare its resulting state with the candidate catalogue. This distinction is important for attribution: a gain caused by a new history encoder is not evidence about the final scoring rule, and a scoring change should be tested without changing the data split or candidate universe.

\subsection{Normalized representations and metric geometry}
Unit-normalized representations have been used to make angular relations explicit in classification and representation learning~\cite{wang2017normface,davidson2018hyperspherical}. Their appeal is not that norms are intrinsically harmful; instead, they impose a modeling restriction that separates direction from scale. In a sequential recommender, the same restriction removes the ability of an item radius to multiply scores across users. Our analysis extends this observation in two ways: Eq.~\ref{eq:reversal} identifies the precise ranking event affected by radial scale, and Eq.~\ref{eq:tangent} characterizes the corresponding training geometry. We consequently evaluate both a fixed-checkpoint score counterfactual and a retrained endpoint, which should not be conflated.

\subsection{Score calibration and frequency effects}
Popularity-aware recommendation studies show that frequency can influence accuracy, exposure, and learned ranking~\cite{steck2011popularity,abdollahpouri2017controlling}. This motivates frequency-stratified evaluation, but it does not imply that an embedding norm is a popularity proxy or that removing norms helps rare targets. We therefore treat popularity only as a diagnostic stratification: active items are partitioned using training interactions, and group results are reported as a boundary of a geometry intervention. \cands{} is neither a popularity penalty nor a tail-augmentation method; it is a controlled change to the final matching geometry.

\section{Method and Analysis}
\subsection{Task and directional calibration}
Let $\mathcal I$ denote the catalogue and let $\bm x_u=(i_1,\ldots,i_L)$ be a chronological prefix for user $u$. A causal sequential encoder $f_\theta$ maps the prefix to $\bm h_u\in\mathbb R^d$, and each item $i$ has an embedding $\bm e_i\in\mathbb R^d$. The conventional full-sort score is $s_{ui}=\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i$. \cands{} replaces it by a temperature-scaled cosine score. For positive next item $i^+$, it uses
\begin{equation}
\ell_{ui}=\tau\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_i,\qquad \mathcal L=-\log\frac{\exp(\ell_{ui^+})}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal I}\exp(\ell_{uj})}.\label{eq:cands}
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat{\bm v}=\bm v/(\|\bm v\|_2+\epsilon)$; $\epsilon$ is only a numerical safeguard. The scalar $\tau>0$ controls softmax concentration and the magnitude of the logit gradient, but it cannot change a within-user ordering because it is shared by all candidates. The method therefore changes neither the history encoder, item identities, sequence length, nor the test-time complexity: full-sort prediction remains one matrix multiplication against the item table.

The implementation supports the same pointwise cross-entropy objective as the SASRec baseline and normalizes the sequence output and the entire item embedding table at each scoring call. It can also be used with the sampled negative objective already supported by the base implementation; the reported experiments use the matched configuration of the baseline. This detail matters because normalization is applied in both training and inference. A test-time cosine re-ranker is a different intervention and is evaluated separately below.

\subsection{Rank invariance and candidate-side asymmetry}
Consider first sequence-only normalization, $s^{\mathrm{seq}}_{ui}=\tau\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i$. For a fixed nonzero state $\bm h_u$, $s^{\mathrm{seq}}_{ui}=\frac{\tau}{\|\bm h_u\|_2}s_{ui}$. The multiplier is positive and independent of $i$; it cannot change the within-user order. This property is exact in real arithmetic and motivates a frozen-representation counterfactual in our experiments.
\begin{proposition}[Sequence-side rank invariance]
For any nonzero $\bm h_u$ and arbitrary candidate vectors $\{\bm e_i\}_{i\in\mathcal I}$, $\operatorname{rank}_i(\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i)=\operatorname{rank}_i(\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i)$, up to ties.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For all candidates $i$, $\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\bm e_i=(1/\|\bm h_u\|_2)\bm h_u^\top\bm e_i$. Multiplication by a common positive scalar preserves every pairwise score inequality.
\end{proof}

Item-side normalization is not invariant in this way because $\|\bm e_i\|_2$ differs across candidates. For target $t$ and competitor $p$, set $c_t=\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_t$ and $c_p=\widehat{\bm h}_u^\top\widehat{\bm e}_p$. If $c_t>c_p>0$, angular scoring chooses $t$ but the dot product chooses $p$ exactly when
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|\bm e_p\|_2}{\|\bm e_t\|_2}>\frac{c_t}{c_p}.\label{eq:reversal}
\end{equation}
The sequence norm cancels, making this a candidate-side radial effect. Eq.~\ref{eq:reversal} is not a superiority claim: candidate radius may encode useful calibration information. It instead establishes why sequence-only and item-only normalization must be analyzed separately.
\begin{proposition}For nonzero representations, \cands{} ranks $t$ above $p$ if and only if $c_t>c_p$, and this order is invariant to independent positive rescaling of the sequence and item vectors.\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}Equation~\ref{eq:cands} is the positive constant $\tau$ times $c_i$. Replacing either vector by a positive multiple leaves its unit vector unchanged. Thus the score difference has the same sign as $c_t-c_p$, independently of all positive radii.\end{proof}
This proposition identifies an invariance, not a superiority theorem. If norm encodes a useful property, dot-product ranking may be preferable. The empirical role of the next sections is to determine whether this restriction helps under matched training.

\subsection{Training geometry}
The rank invariance above is not an optimization invariance. For the dot-product full-softmax loss, with $p_{uj}$ denoting the softmax probability and $y_{uj}=\mathbf{1}[j=i^+]$, the item gradient is $\nabla_{\bm e_j}\mathcal L=(p_{uj}-y_{uj})\bm h_u$. Its radial projection is generally nonzero. Likewise, scaling $\bm h_u$ changes dot-product logits without changing its direction, so the sequence norm acts as a sample-dependent logit scale during training.
\begin{proposition}[Tangential normalized-score gradient]
Let $\bm z=\bm v/\|\bm v\|_2$ and let $\mathcal L$ be any differentiable loss that depends on $\bm v$ only through $\bm z$. Then
\begin{equation}
\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L=\frac{1}{\|\bm v\|_2}\left(\bm I-\bm z\bm z^\top\right)\nabla_{\bm z}\mathcal L,
\qquad \bm v^\top\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L=0.\label{eq:tangent}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}For nonzero $\bm v$, differentiating $\bm z=\bm v/\|\bm v\|_2$ gives the Jacobian $\|\bm v\|_2^{-1}(\bm I-\bm z\bm z^\top)$. Applying the chain rule yields the first equality. The matrix in parentheses is an orthogonal projection onto the tangent space of the unit sphere, hence it annihilates $\bm z$. Since $\bm v=\|\bm v\|_2\bm z$, the inner product with $\nabla_{\bm v}\mathcal L$ is zero.\end{proof}
Equation~\ref{eq:tangent} applies to $\bm v=\bm h_u$ for sequence-only normalization and to both $\bm h_u$ and $\bm e_i$ for joint normalization. Thus, sequence normalization can change training even though it cannot change the frozen ranking in Proposition~1. The $1/\|\bm v\|_2$ factor also changes directional update scale in the unconstrained parameterization. This does not guarantee higher metrics: radius can contain useful information, and other paths through the encoder or optimizer can affect norms indirectly. Matched endpoint tests and direct gradient diagnostics are consequently both necessary.

\begin{proposition}[Cosine-logit capacity]
For a catalogue of $M$ items and temperature-scaled cosine logits
$\ell_{ui}\in[-\tau,\tau]$, the per-example full-softmax cross-entropy loss
is bounded below by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{u}\geq \log\!\left(1+(M-1)e^{-2\tau}\right).\label{eq:logitbound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The positive logit cannot exceed $\tau$, while every negative logit is at
least $-\tau$. The largest attainable positive softmax probability is therefore
$[1+(M-1)e^{-2\tau}]^{-1}$. Taking its negative logarithm gives
Eq.~\ref{eq:logitbound}.
\end{proof}
This bound makes the role of temperature explicit. Unit normalization bounds all logit gaps by $2\tau$; $\tau$ is therefore an explicit capacity and calibration control, not an incidental hyperparameter. It cannot alter a fixed user's angular ordering, but it changes loss concentration and gradients during training.

\subsection{Falsifiable checkpoint diagnostics}
We use three diagnostics, each with a different falsifiable prediction. First, for a fixed representation we compare dot scores with sequence-normalized scores under the identical history mask. Proposition~1 predicts identical target ranks and top-$K$ lists up to numerical ties. We also normalize only item embeddings; its lower top-$K$ overlap quantifies the candidate-side asymmetry rather than treating it as an optimization effect.

Second, we measure radial-gradient energy at the final matching head, as well as the empirical score standard deviation, predictive entropy, and top-one/top-two margin after training. Equation~\ref{eq:tangent} predicts a zero direct radial component for normalized inputs; the distributional measures test whether the learned models also differ in score concentration.

Third, we compare paired rank transitions between dot and normalized checkpoints. For every test target, we record movements among $1$--$5$, $6$--$10$, $11$--$20$, $21$--$50$, $51$--$100$, and $>100$. This diagnostic is essential for interpreting a metric trade-off: a model can improve deep retrieval while failing to improve top-$10$ ranking. None of these diagnostics is a causal claim about data generation; jointly, they distinguish a frozen-score property from a retrained endpoint effect.

For every eligible user, the latest interaction is held out for test and the preceding interaction for validation; all earlier interactions form training prefixes. We evaluate by full-sort ranking over the complete catalogue after masking observed training items according to the standard sequential-evaluation protocol. For a target rank $r_u$, $\mathrm{Recall@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal U|}\sum_u\mathbb{1}[r_u\leq K]$ and $\mathrm{NDCG@}K=\frac{1}{|\mathcal U|}\sum_u\mathbb{1}[r_u\leq K]/\log_2(r_u+1)$. We report both metrics at cutoffs $5$, $10$, $20$, $50$, and $100$, using NDCG@10 as the primary compact metric in the main tables. Head, mid, and tail are global, training-only partitions of active items sorted by training interaction count. The groups contain equal numbers of \emph{items}, not equal numbers of test examples; this prevents test leakage and avoids changing a target's group according to the test split.

\subsection{Matched comparison}
\sasrec{} and \cands{} share architecture, chronological split, optimizer, batch size, early stopping rule, and random seed. The base setup has two Transformer layers, 256 hidden units, two attention heads, inner size 1024, and maximum history length 50. The only intended method difference is the scoring geometry in Eq.~\ref{eq:cands}. Three seeds (2023, 2024, and 2025) are matched one-to-one. We report their mean and standard deviation, paired seed deltas, and the fraction of positive deltas. A checkpoint and a temperature are selected by validation only; the test set is not used to choose either. We distinguish a one-seed temperature-development split from independent-seed endpoint confirmation.

The comparison is intentionally narrow. It establishes whether directional calibration improves a strong self-attentive baseline under controlled conditions; it does not establish dominance over every recent sequential model. We state this scope because changing the encoder, negative-sampling regime, or preprocessing while changing the score geometry would make an apparent gain hard to interpret.

\subsection{Mechanism protocol and reproducibility}
For each fixed test state, our diagnostic masks histories identically before comparing raw dot, sequence-normalized, and item-normalized scores. It records rank equality, top-$K$ overlap, score scale, entropy, margins, and transitions between rank bins. This is a frozen-score diagnostic, not a causal claim about retraining. We separately test the tangential-gradient prediction by differentiating only the final matching head, holding the complementary side fixed. Every endpoint record stores the dataset, seed, checkpoint path, and command; the released scripts separate training, validation-only tuning, and checkpoint analysis. This separation prevents an analysis script from silently altering a completed result.

\subsection{Mechanism evidence}
The decisive test of Proposition~1 uses the same frozen representation, history mask, target, and candidate catalogue. Across three Beauty seeds, replacing raw dot scores by sequence-normalized scores preserved the target rank for $99.91\%$--$99.96\%$ of test examples and preserved the entire top-$10$ list for approximately $99.99\%$. The small residual is attributable to finite-precision comparisons at near ties. Thus, a sequence-only score substitution is not a plausible explanation for the endpoint gain.

The same counterfactual exposes the candidate-side asymmetry. Normalizing item vectors reduced raw-versus-item-normalized top-$10$ Jaccard overlap to .71--.79, depending on the trained checkpoint. Item normalization consequently changes the candidate list, whereas sequence normalization does not. This distinction is why joint normalization should be evaluated with a separate temperature rather than described as a stronger version of the same inference-time operation.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} also shows that normalized training is associated with less dispersed scores, higher predictive entropy, and smaller top-one/top-two gaps. These quantities do not prove a unique causal path from score dispersion to NDCG; they are distributional evidence consistent with explicit temperature calibration replacing unconstrained radial scaling.

\subsection{Direct gradient check and external decomposition}
The gradient prediction is observed directly rather than inferred from final embedding norms. Holding the candidate table fixed, the radial-energy fraction of the final sequence-state gradient was 3.25\% on average for dot-product SASRec across Beauty seeds, and numerically zero ($<4\times10^{-16}$) for sequence-only and joint normalization. Holding the encoder output fixed gives the corresponding item-side result: it is near zero for joint normalization but not for sequence-only normalization. These measurements match the asymmetric derivation in Eq.~\ref{eq:tangent}: sequence-only normalization constrains the sequence scoring path, while joint normalization constrains both final-score paths.

Temperature is necessary for this constraint to remain useful. A wide Beauty development grid over $\{.5,1,2,5,10,20,40\}$ selected $\tau=5$ for sequence-only and $\tau=10$ for joint normalization. A local validation-only refinement selected $\tau=4$ for sequence-only (NDCG@10=.0605) but did not improve the joint $\tau=10$ optimum (NDCG@10=.0608). Independent seeds confirmed that sequence $\tau=4$ and $\tau=5$ occupy a stable region: $\tau=4$ increased NDCG@10 by .0003 while decreasing Recall@10 by .0016. We therefore report a stable region rather than claiming a universal point optimum.

The paired rank-transition analysis clarifies the frequency boundary. For tail targets, sequence-only and joint normalization improved Recall@50 by .0088 and .0176, respectively, but changed Recall@10 by $-.0020$ and $-.0011$. Joint normalization moved 2.58\% of tail targets from ranks above 50 into ranks 11--50, yet its top-$10$ entry rate (.69\%) remained below its top-$10$ exit rate (.81\%). The geometry therefore improves coarse retrieval for some deep targets without guaranteeing their fine top-$K$ order.

We then use the pre-specified Yelp decomposition as an external check beyond short Amazon purchase sequences. Joint normalization is best on every reported Yelp metric, whereas item-only normalization is below the dot product. Thus the result supports neither a generic ``smaller norm'' explanation nor the view that normalizing items alone is sufficient. The dot-product L2 control also does not match joint normalization. These observations support joint score geometry as an empirical option while retaining sequence-scale calibration as the mechanism isolated by the frozen-rank theorem.

\subsection{Sensitivity and failure modes}
The current evidence does not support a transferable point value of $\tau$. The Beauty development experiment identifies an interior effective region, while the external experiments retain their independently selected protocol. Temperature must be tuned on validation data because its role is to set loss concentration after radial scale is constrained; selecting it on test data would invalidate the mechanism comparison.

The matched seeds identify three failure modes. First, a post-hoc sequence-cosine replacement is rank-invariant and cannot reproduce a retrained endpoint gain. Second, item-only normalization is not a safe proxy for joint normalization because it removes candidate-specific scale from the ordering. Third, tail NDCG can decrease even when overall NDCG and deep recall improve. A practitioner seeking tail coverage should therefore add tail-specific evidence, objectives, or constraints rather than treating score normalization as a substitute for them.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Ethics}
The central interpretation is narrower than a generic claim for cosine similarity. The endpoint results, direct radial-gradient check, temperature sweep, and frozen-state counterfactual jointly support a training-geometry explanation for the observed aggregate gain. The strongest logical link is negative: because sequence-only normalization preserves the fixed ranking, the gain cannot be attributed to a post-hoc sequence re-ranker. The gradient and distributional diagnostics then identify how training differs. They do not establish that score dispersion alone causes NDCG gains, or that a norm is a nuisance variable in every recommender.

The frequency result provides a concrete boundary. \cands{} neither reconstructs missing tail evidence nor guarantees tail exposure. The transition analysis shows that angular geometry can improve deep retrieval while failing to resolve local top-$K$ competition among sparse candidates. Items with few interactions may need content, graph structure, uncertainty estimates, or explicitly constrained objectives; none is supplied by directional calibration. The practical role of \cands{} is therefore a low-complexity geometric baseline or component, not a replacement for a tail-specific method.

There are also threats to validity. The fixed-checkpoint comparison controls the sequence state, history mask, examples, and candidate universe but cannot identify a causal data-generation process. The current study uses one encoder family, three Amazon domains plus Yelp, fixed hidden width, and a validation-selected temperature protocol. It reports both matched-seed variation and, on Beauty, within-seed paired user bootstrap intervals. These design choices make the present attribution cleaner but limit external generalization. A submission-ready extension should test more encoder families and public benchmarks.

We use anonymous public interaction IDs, no sensitive attributes, no review text, and no synthetic behavior. Data redistribution follows the original source terms. Offline relevance metrics do not measure marketplace exposure, seller impact, or user welfare; any deployment would require coverage, fairness, and stakeholder analyses beyond the scope of this study.

\section{Conclusion}
\cands{} makes a small but revealing change to sequential recommendation: it constrains final matching to temperature-controlled angular compatibility. We proved that sequence normalization is rank-invariant for a fixed state, established its tangential training geometry, and used matched endpoints, validation-only temperature selection, direct gradients, frozen-score counterfactuals, and rank transitions to test the resulting mechanism. The evidence supports an optimization-geometry and calibration benefit for overall sequential ranking, not a universal cosine or tail-recommendation claim. This framing makes the limitation useful: remaining top-$K$ failures identify missing fine-grained evidence rather than motivating another untested score transformation.
